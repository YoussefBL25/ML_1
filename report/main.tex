\begin{abstract}
We predict coronary heart disease from 328,135 answers to the 2015 BRFSS
survey, 8.8\% of them positive, and compare four least-squares models with
plain and regularized logistic regression by nested cross-validation of F1.
Logistic regression beats the linear models in every fold, $0.432$ against
at most $0.420$. Our final model, regularized logistic regression with
pairwise interactions of the 40 most important features, reaches
$0.441\pm0.006$, and $0.454$ on AIcrowd. Preprocessing with the BRFSS
codebook gives the largest gain, whereas regularization and class weighting
do not help a model that underfits.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death worldwide. We predict
self-reported coronary heart disease or myocardial infarction (MICHD) from the
2015 BRFSS telephone survey, a binary task with strong class imbalance, scored
with F1 and solved with NumPy only. We contribute (a) a label-free
preprocessing built from the BRFSS codebook (Section~\ref{sec:data}); (b) a
nested cross-validation protocol in which the threshold is also chosen without
seeing the test fold (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}); (c) a comparison of linear
and logistic models and of class weighting, and a final model with
interactions (Sections~\ref{sec:methods}--\ref{sec:final}); and (d) ablations
showing which choices actually improve F1 (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
Models are evaluated by nested cross-validation over five stratified folds
shared by all experiments. For each test fold, the four other folds
estimate the preprocessing statistics and, by an inner 3-fold
cross-validation, select the hyperparameters and the threshold $t$ that
maximize F1; the selected model is retrained on them and evaluated on the
test fold, which thus never influences a choice. We report the mean and standard deviation of F1 over the test
folds. Two configurations are compared by their per-fold F1 difference
$\Delta$, and one is better only if $\Delta>0$ in all five folds. The
submitted model results from the same selection on the full training set.

% ============================================================================
% OWNER: Person 3 (linear methods)
% ============================================================================
\subsection{Linear models}
The linear models regress $y_i\in\{0,1\}$ on the features with the mean
squared error $L(w)=\frac1{2N}\|y-Xw\|^2$, and threshold the score
$x_i^\top w$ like a probability. Least squares solves the normal equations;
ridge regression minimizes $L(w)+\lambda\|w\|^2$,
$\lambda\in\{10^{-6},\dots,10^{-1}\}$. From $w=0$, gradient descent (GD)
takes 200 full-batch steps, $\gamma\in\{1,3,10,30,60,90\}\cdot10^{-3}$, and
SGD 200{,}000 single-sample steps, about one epoch, with seed 0 and
$\gamma\in\{1,3\}\cdot10^{-6},\dots,\{1,3\}\cdot10^{-4}$. The threshold grid
is the one of the logistic models.

% ============================================================================
% OWNER: Person 2 (logistic models, imbalance, final model)
% ============================================================================
\subsection{Logistic models}
Logistic regression models the probability of disease as
$\sigma(x_i^\top w)$, with $y_i\in\{0,1\}$, and minimizes the mean negative
log-likelihood
$L(w)=\frac1N\sum_i\bigl[\log(1+e^{x_i^\top w})-y_i x_i^\top w\bigr]$; its
regularized version minimizes $L(w)+\lambda\|w\|^2$. Both use full-batch
gradient descent with step size $\gamma$ from $w=0$ for 300 iterations,
after which the loss changes by less than $0.06\%$. The inner
cross-validation selects $\gamma\in\{0.01,0.03,0.1,0.3,1\}$,
$\lambda\in\{10^{-6},\dots,10^{-1}\}$ and $t\in\{0.05,0.06,\dots,0.95\}$.

\subsection{Final model}\label{sec:final}
A linear model cannot capture joint effects, such as smoking being more
dangerous for older respondents. The final model therefore fits regularized
logistic regression, the best method (Table~\ref{tab:methods}), ranks the
standardized features by $|w_j|$, and refits it with the $K(K-1)/2$
standardized products of the top $K$, with $K\in\{20,30,\dots,80\}$ chosen
by 3-fold cross-validation.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
The final model is the best method (Table~\ref{tab:methods}), with F1
$=0.441\pm0.006$ in nested cross-validation and $0.454$ on AIcrowd. Its
gain does not come from the hyperparameters: F1 is flat around the selected
$\gamma=0.3$, $\lambda=10^{-4}$ and $K=40$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:tuning}), and
every outer fold selects $\gamma\in\{0.3,1\}$ and $\lambda\le10^{-3}$. Nor
does it come from regularization: plain and regularized logistic regression
are tied, $\Delta=0.0003\pm0.0014$, as training and validation losses of
$0.219$ and $0.221$ show no overfitting.
The linear models are weaker: least squares and ridge reach F1 $0.420$,
and regularized logistic regression beats least squares in all five folds,
$\Delta=0.011\pm0.002$, mostly through precision, $0.358$ against $0.346$.
The squared loss also penalizes correct predictions beyond 0 or 1, so
well-classified respondents distort the fit, which the log-likelihood
avoids. As for logistic regression, $\lambda\le10^{-3}$ changes F1 by less
than $0.001$. GD, with $\gamma=0.09$ in every fold, comes within $0.002$ of
least squares; SGD, limited to one epoch, loses $0.005$.

The gain therefore comes from the features, added one at a time in
Table~\ref{tab:ablation}. The codebook rules contribute most, raising
precision from $0.337$ to $0.361$. One-hot encoding and missing indicators
add $0.004$ and $0.005$ in all folds, the interactions $0.003$ in four folds
only, a likely but uncertain gain. Squared or binned features, more
interactions and model averaging changed F1 by less than $0.001$.

Finally, the class imbalance needs no correction beyond the threshold.
Weighting the loss of the positive samples by $\omega=10.3$, the ratio of
negatives to positives, or by its square root $3.2$ gives F1 $0.430$ and
$0.431$ against $0.432$, but thresholds $0.75$ and $0.48$ instead of
$0.21$. Weighting multiplies the odds by about $\omega$ without changing
the order of the predictions, so only the threshold moves: for
$\omega=10.3$, $0.21$ becomes $0.73$.

\section{Conclusion}
The final model, regularized logistic regression with interactions, reaches
a nested-CV F1 of $0.441\pm0.006$ and $0.454$ on AIcrowd. The codebook rules
give the largest gain, the model does not overfit, so regularization does
not help, and threshold tuning already handles the imbalance. The labels are
self-reported and the survey is observational, so the model captures
associations, not causes, and with a precision of $0.37$ most predicted
positives are false alarms. The small, uncertain gain of the interactions
suggests that linear models are close to their limit on these features.
